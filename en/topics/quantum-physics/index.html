Quantum physics

Quantum physics read differently: to measure is to project

The short answer

In quantum physics, to measure is to project: the state is read on the axis of the instrument, and the projection loses part of what it reads. What is called chance, collapse or half-spin then reads as the price of that reading, not as a mystery of matter.

What physics establishes

FactIn the standard formalism, measuring an observable on a state yields one of its eigenvalues, with probability equal to the square of the state's projection on the corresponding direction; the state after measurement is that projection. Projection is the central operation of every quantum measurement.

FactThe electron and other fermions have spin ½ (Dirac, 1928): they must be turned 720°, not 360°, to return to their initial state.

The ARC reading

ReadingProjecting a vector onto an axis is exactly the operation quantum mechanics calls measurement. The difference between geometry and quantum measurement lies in the interpretive frame, not in the operation: all observable geometry is projective, hence quantum.

ReadingRandomness is not in nature; it is the residue of projecting a sphere onto two values. The wave does not collapse: it changes reading.

ReadingSpin ½ is the constant k = ½ of the nine-digit grid, the barycentre 4.5 over 9: the cost of reading a three-dimensional state through a two-dimensional measurement. The 720° are two turns of reading for one observed turn.

TheoremApplied to entanglement, this reading recovers exactly the quantum correlation −cos θ and the Tsirelson bound 2√2.
